Mars has a volcano almost three times the height of Everest, a canyon as long as the United States and craters everywhere. Press a shape into sand, and find the place on Mars that looks like it.

How it works.

Three steps from a handful of sand to a real place on Mars.

  1. Step 1

    Sculpt.

    Press hills, ridges and valleys into sand or modelling clay. Big, clear shapes work best.

  2. Step 2

    Photograph.

    Take one photo from straight above. A depth model turns it into a height map, right in your browser.

  3. Step 3

    Found.

    Your shape is compared with 102,915 real places, turned through every angle, and shown on satellite imagery and in 3D.

Tested, not just pretty.

We take real places on Mars, bend them into rough, rotated, blurry copies like a hand-made sculpture, and check whether the search traces each one back.

About the project.

An experiment where GIS, computer vision and planetary remote sensing meet: a physical sculpture goes in, a real place on Mars comes out.

Why it exists.

Projected sandboxes can draw contour lines on sand, and tangible GIS tools can rebuild a known place in sand. None of them asks the reverse question: given a shape made by hand, where on Mars is it?

What's inside.

Depth Anything V2 reads the photo as a height map. A neural network trained on thousands of real landscapes picks the candidates. Every candidate is then turned through all angles and compared exactly, which also tells you which way your sandbox would face on the map.

A fair map of the planet.

Web Mercator stretches the poles, so pixel-sized windows make the polar regions win far too often. Here every window is measured in kilometres, giving every latitude the same chance.

Where the data comes from.

Elevation from the MGS MOLA laser altimeter (NASA, 1.85 km grid). Imagery from Viking and MOLA via NASA Mars Trek. Place names from the IAU Gazetteer of Planetary Nomenclature.
